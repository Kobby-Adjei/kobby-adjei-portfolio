Case one · Product design · FlmLnk

The product read as generated, not made.

FlmLnk is a marketing tool for filmmakers. It worked. But it wore the costume of every other AI app that shipped that year — the sparkle avatar, the rocket icon, the co-pilot tagline — and filmmakers could feel it.

So I stopped treating it as a list of ugly screens and started treating it as one problem with one cause. What follows is the frame I used, then five changes that came out of it, in the order they taught me something.

The frame: one thesis, five principles

Everything below traces back to a single sentence:

Every change removes something that made the product feel unsure of itself. The thesis the whole renovation hangs on

That framing did more work than any individual fix. It converted a hundred small aesthetic arguments into one question anyone on the team could answer: does this make the product look like it knows what it is? A borrowed look is a borrowed identity.

Underneath it sit five principles. Every entry in the log is tagged to one of them, which is what stops a renovation drifting into personal preference:

  1. Brand carries the screen. Remove the generic AI signifiers; let the reel mark, the serif, and the poster art do the talking.
  2. Clarity is a feature. Anything that neither informs nor steps aside is noise — and the user came here to spend attention on their film.
  3. Acknowledge instantly. Feedback latency reads as breakage; never make the user wonder if they were heard.
  4. Personality is felt, not noticed. Texture and warmth up to, but never past, the point where they compete with the content.
  5. One accent, one voice. Consistency is what makes a product read as trustworthy.
How the work is recordedlayer two

The renovation is an append-only log, not a document that gets rewritten. Every shipped change gets an entry in the same shape — screen and context, the problem before, the change after, why it matters, and the principle it traces to — plus a before/after frame pair sharing a base filename so the two can always be lined up.

Twenty-one entries so far, grouped into chapters: individual screen fixes, a cross-screen redundancy audit, the Flow chat movement, and the public page movement. Deep changes get a case study of their own that the log points to.

The reason for the discipline is boring and important. By the tenth change the argument was already shorter, because the principles had been settled once instead of re-litigated every time.

1 · Where it started: three mental models on one screen

The home screen was the first entry, and it set the pattern for everything after — the problem was never decoration, it was structure.

The diagnosis that mattered: this wasn’t ugly, it was three mental models stacked on one screen — a guided wizard, an open canvas, and a dashboard. You cannot fix that with spacing.

The slate answers it in the product’s own language. One object, one path, and a film-set metaphor doing the work that three competing containers were failing to do.

2 · Not every fix is a visual one

The next entry is the one I would point a hiring manager at, because it isn’t a redesign. It’s an architecture call, logged in the same place as the visual work.

Same steps, same content — but a full page tells the truth about how long the task takes and how much it matters. The give-away is in the before: the step rail is squeezed and the content scrolls inside the card. The container was already losing an argument with the work.

3 · The centrepiece: the Flow chat

The assistant is the surface a filmmaker actually talks to, so it got a full movement rather than a patch — five logged entries across identity, clarity, trust, comfort and focus. This is where the thesis is most visible.

The change I care about most isn’t visible in any screenshot. The thinking indicator used to fire only after a database round-trip and the model’s first token — so when you hit send, nothing happened for a beat. That beat is where a user decides the product is broken.

Now it fires the instant you send, before any network work, and the reel spins while it thinks. Nothing about the model got faster. The product just stopped leaving people in doubt — and the loading moment became a brand moment instead of a dead one.

The principle doing the work

Feedback latency reads as breakage even when the work is fine. It is the cheapest trust win in most products, and it is almost always left on the floor.

The full Flow chat movement — five entrieslayer two

07 · Identity. Sparkle avatar → reel mark. Rocket dropped from Upgrade, swapped to an eye on Preview, because a rocket means launch, not look. Blue image badge and accent bar → film-red. Co-pilot tagline removed. The three in-chat sparkles became a telescope (researching), a spinner (thinking), and a plus (generate). Asset and poster cards given sharp corners under a “soft chrome, sharp posters” rule, so a poster reads as a poster and not an app tile.

08 · Clarity. Image cards were printing the raw generation prompt as their caption — identical across a whole row, burying the poster art. Captions removed; the poster is the information, and the prompt moved to the detail view. Opening a chat used to save an empty session immediately, so history filled with indistinguishable “New Chat” rows; a chat is now a draft until the first message. Zero counts hidden.

09 · Trust. The thinking indicator moved ahead of the save round-trip and the first token. The redundant pill spinner was removed.

10 · Comfort. The conversation sat on a flat black void that read as a placeholder. Added a faint dot stipple behind the conversation only — never the welcome screen, never over content — and made message bubbles opaque so texture can’t bleed through text.

11 · Focus. The conversation was pinned to a narrow centred column that wasted the panel. Widened to a fitted measure that reclaims the room without letting lines run off the eye, and added a collapse-to-rail toggle for the history sidebar that remembers the choice instead of resetting it.

4 · When a form should have been a sentence

By this point the principles were settled enough to attack something harder than styling. The email composer was the densest screen in the product.

The controls didn’t disappear. They became grammar. Same options, rearranged into something a person already knows how to read, so the screen teaches itself instead of demanding to be learned. That is clarity is a feature applied to interaction rather than to decoration.

5 · The surface other people see

The last one matters for a different reason. Everything above is seen by the filmmaker. This one is seen by their audience.

A published film page carries more reputational weight per pixel than anything else in the product, because a filmmaker sends it to people they want to impress. Showing the artefact instead of describing it is the whole change.

Where it is today

Captured from live production while writing this, so you get the honest state rather than a curated one:

What I’d defend, and what I wouldn’t

The log is the real artefact. Any designer can produce a nicer screen. Keeping twenty-one changes traceable to five principles is what turns that into a system somebody else can extend without me in the room — and it is why the tenth change took less argument than the first.

What’s missing is measurement. This case is reasoned, not tested. I can show you that the thinking indicator now fires before the network call, and argue from what is known about feedback latency that it matters — but I have not run the study that proves the trust win on these users. The honest claim is a better-argued product, not a proven one.

Which is exactly the gap the second case closes. There I had to stop arguing and start measuring — and the measurement caught me being wrong.
