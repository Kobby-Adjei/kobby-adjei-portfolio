How do you rank 4,000 films without 16 million comparisons?

FlmLnk's engine decides which old films in a catalogue deserve a marketing test. It watches two trailers, picks the stronger film, and does that for every pair, shown both ways round so the order it sees them in cannot sway it. A batch of 30 films takes 870 comparisons, about 48 minutes. Real catalogues run into the thousands.

The problem

Comparing every pair grows with the square of the catalogue. Double the films and the work goes up four times. At 4,000 films that is 15,996,000 comparisons. The engine runs at 18 model calls a minute, so ranking one catalogue would take 617 days.

Splitting the catalogue into batches of 30 does not fix it. A film's score is the share of matchups it won inside its own batch, so it only means something next to the films it met. 134 batches give you 134 separate leaderboards. A weak film in a weak batch outscores a strong film in a strong one, and nothing tells you which is which.

The idea: a fixed ladder

Put the same reference films into every batch. If every film in the catalogue is measured against the same rungs, every score lands on one scale, and the batches join up into a single ranking. Standardised tests use the same trick: they carry a set of anchor questions between exam versions so a score means the same thing from year to year.

The catch is choosing the rungs. You want them spread from the best film to the worst, but on a catalogue you have never seen you do not know where the best and worst are. So the ladder is built from the catalogue itself.

How it works

Rank a random 30. Random because nothing is known yet, so a hand picked sample would only be a guess. Every film plays every other, which gives a small, properly ordered ladder built from this customer's own films.

Keep six as reference films. The best, the worst, and four spread evenly between. Deliberately not the top six: if every reference were a hit, every film would lose to all of them and they would all score the same. Only films the engine judges the same way on two separate runs get kept, because the six are reused thousands of times and any wobble in them spreads into every score.

Screen everything against the six. Each remaining film plays the same six matches and lands in a band. A film that loses to any reference is not getting a marketing budget, so it is finished and costs nothing more.

Run a real contest among the survivors. A film has to beat the best of 30 random films to get through, which about 1 in 31 films will. At 4,000 films that is roughly 130, and those get a full round robin. That is the part that needs to be precise, because that is where the money goes. It is the same shape search engines use: a cheap pass over everything, then an expensive pass on the few candidates that matter.

Where it stands

This is a design with its arithmetic worked out, not a measured result. The engine in production still compares every pair and takes 30 films a batch. The next step is building the reference set into the engine, then running it against the full pair by pair ranking on the same films and comparing the shortlists, how stable they are across runs, what fails, and what it costs.

Two things the method does not solve. At 18 calls a minute, 65,022 comparisons is still about two and a half days, so the call rate is the next ceiling. And the final is sized by how many films a customer will actually fund, which is a business decision, not something the algorithm can pick for them.

You do not need to put 4,000 films in order. You need to find the few worth money. Those are different problems, and the second one is far cheaper.
