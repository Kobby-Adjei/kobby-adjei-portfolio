The shortcut that saved 99.6% of the work, and lost 4 of the 10 best films

FlmLnk's engine decides which old films in a catalogue deserve a marketing test. It watches two trailers, picks the stronger film, and does that for every pair, shown both ways round so the order it sees them in cannot sway it. A batch of 30 films takes 870 comparisons, about 48 minutes. Real catalogues run into the thousands.

The problem

Comparing every pair grows with the square of the catalogue. Double the films and the work goes up four times. At 4,000 films that is 15,996,000 comparisons. The engine runs at 18 model calls a minute, so ranking one catalogue would take 617 days.

Splitting the catalogue into batches of 30 does not fix it. A film's score is the share of matchups it won inside its own batch, so it only means something next to the films it met. 134 batches give you 134 separate leaderboards. A weak film in a weak batch outscores a strong film in a strong one, and nothing tells you which is which.

The idea: a fixed ladder

Put the same reference films into every batch. If every film in the catalogue is measured against the same rungs, every score lands on one scale, and the batches join up into a single ranking. Standardised tests use the same trick: they carry a set of anchor questions between exam versions so a score means the same thing from year to year.

The catch is choosing the rungs. You want them spread from the best film to the worst, but on a catalogue you have never seen you do not know where the best and worst are. So the ladder is built from the catalogue itself.

How it works

Rank a random 30. Random because nothing is known yet, so a hand picked sample would only be a guess. Every film plays every other, which gives a small, properly ordered ladder built from this customer's own films.

Keep six as reference films. The best, the worst, and four spread evenly between. Deliberately not the top six: if every reference were a hit, every film would lose to all of them and they would all score the same. Only films the engine judges the same way on two separate runs get kept, because the six are reused thousands of times and any wobble in them spreads into every score.

Screen everything against the six. Each remaining film plays the same six matches and lands in a band. A film that loses to any reference is not getting a marketing budget, so it is finished and costs nothing more.

Run a real contest among the survivors. A film has to beat the best of 30 random films to get through, which about 1 in 31 films will. At 4,000 films that is roughly 130, and those get a full round robin. That is the part that needs to be precise, because that is where the money goes. It is the same shape search engines use: a cheap pass over everything, then an expensive pass on the few candidates that matter.

So I tested it

The arithmetic was never in doubt. The question was whether a film's record against six fixed opponents says the same thing as its record against the whole field. We already had the answer to check against: 62 films, each compared with about fourteen others, run twice.

So I pretended to know nothing about those films, froze six as a ladder, screened the other 55 against only those six, and compared the result with the thorough ranking.

Why it fails

The first ladder failed in a way that named the cause. Here are three real records:

The best film in the catalogue scored below a film ranked 33rd. Four of the six references were so weak that every film worth considering beat them, so those four rungs answered "yes" every time and carried no information. Six rungs, two of them useful.

So I built the opposite ladder, six strong films, and it broke the same way in the other direction: nearly everything lost to nearly every rung, and the films bunched at the bottom instead of the top. Then twelve references, and the score did not move at all.

That last result is the real finding. Each comparison the model makes agrees with our reviewers about 80% of the time, so every reading carries noise. Asking a film the same six questions twice over does not cancel that noise out. The thorough ranking works because every film meets a different set of opponents, so the errors land in different places instead of repeating.

Where it stands

The simple version is dead: screen a catalogue against a fixed ladder, take the top, ship it. What might survive is the two-stage shape, a cheap pass that keeps a generous band followed by a real contest inside it. The best ladder held all ten of the top films, but only inside 47% of the catalogue, and half of 4,000 films is still far too many for the second stage to be worth running.

Two things I would keep in mind reading any of this. The ranking I measured against is our own thorough ranking, not the truth, so what I know is that the two disagree on four films, not which one is wrong. And six of ten is well above chance, which is about two, so the signal is real and merely insufficient.

Separately, the constraint that started all of this turned out to be imaginary. The engine throttled itself to 18 model calls a minute because of a note written against a model we no longer use. I ramped it against the live configuration and it ran 240 a minute with no rate limiting at all, so the ceiling is at least thirteen times what we were using. Time was never the real limit. Cost is: about $700 a catalogue with the shortcut, around $171,000 without it, which is why a cheaper method is still worth finding.

The arithmetic was the easy part. A method that saves 99.6% of the work and loses 4 of your 10 best films has not saved you anything.
