The reference was the 2012 Dollar Shave Club launch ad. I don't host its audio here. You can hear it on the official upload. It is a comedy ad, so we borrowed its rhythm, not its tone.

How I measured it

I built the film with Claude, an AI coding assistant. Claude cannot hear audio. So every voice choice rested on my ears alone, and my ears could say "robotic" but not why. My idea was to measure a narration that sounds natural and tune ours to match. Claude built the measuring tool. It scores five things:

  • Pace. How fast the words come.
  • Pauses. How long the breaks are, and how many are long breaks between ideas.
  • Phrasing. Words between breaths. Too many is a run-on. Too few is choppy.
  • Melody. How far the pitch rises and falls. Flat sounds robotic.
  • Emphasis. How much the loudness swings.

It then gives one score for how far a take is from the ad. Numbers turned "sounds robotic" into something I could fix and check. The picture below shows what the numbers saw.

Turning points

  1. The music was sad. I said the piano bed was "lowkey a sad vibe". Instead of guessing again, Claude made three short previews in different styles. I picked indie electronic.
  2. "Doesn't sound like a real campaign." The narrator read the script like a script. Claude rewrote every line to sound spoken, and I picked the take that worked.
  3. The render cache. Every small change redrew about 8,000 frames. I asked Claude to cache what it could. It stored each scene under a fingerprint, and a test caught a bug where one edit redrew every scene.
  4. The script rules. While rewriting the film for investors, I kept making the same corrections. Claude turned them into eight saved rules. Two of them: define every term, and every number needs a because.
  5. Measure a real ad. My idea, because Claude cannot hear. Claude suggested three references. I chose the Dollar Shave Club ad.
  6. The flat pitch. The numbers explained what I had been hearing. The old voice moved over about 9 semitones, against 14.5 in the ad.
  7. The run-on. A new long take scored well overall. I said it still sounded like a run-on. The numbers had already shown it: 10% long breaks against the ad's 34%. The good melody hid it in the average. Claude said it had missed this.
  8. The fix. Record each sentence on its own. Bring every line to the same loudness. Join them with gaps we set: 0.75 to 0.85 seconds between ideas, 0.35 to 0.4 inside one. Then each measure has to pass on its own, and speech-to-text checks every word.

The nerdy part

For anyone who wants the details. The measuring tool is a short Python script.

  • Word timings. faster-whisper, a speech-to-text model, gives the start and end of every word. A gap of 0.15 seconds or more counts as a pause. Using word times means background music cannot fake a pause.
  • Pace. Words divided by talking time.
  • Pauses. The typical pause is the median. Long breaks are the share of pauses over 0.6 seconds. This is the run-on test.
  • Phrasing. Words divided by the number of phrases, which is pauses plus one.
  • Melody. Praat, a speech analysis tool, tracks the pitch every 10 ms, only inside words. Each value becomes semitones from the speaker's middle pitch. The range is the 90th percentile minus the 10th, so a few stray points do not count.
  • Emphasis. How much the loudness inside words varies, in decibels. Near-silent frames are ignored.
  • Distance. Each measure's gap from the ad is divided by what we count as one typical miss, for example 2 semitones of melody or 0.4 words a second of pace. Then each gap is squared, the squares are averaged, and the distance is the square root of that. 0 means identical. The ad is measured over its first 75 seconds, the part with talking.

The fixes

  • Line by line. Each sentence is recorded as its own file, with the same voice and settings. Act 1 was 12 recordings. The model never sees the next line, so it cannot run into it.
  • Same loudness per line. Each line gets one fixed volume change. A dynamic normaliser would pump up the breaths and gaps.
  • Trim and speed. Each line is sped up 12% with the pitch kept, and its own silence is cut from both ends.
  • Set pauses. 0.75 to 0.85 seconds between ideas. 0.35 to 0.4 seconds inside one.
  • Room tone. The gaps hold faint noise, not digital silence. Real recordings always have some, and dead silence also threw off the emphasis number.
  • Pass marks. Each measure has to pass on its own, not just the overall score. Speech-to-text then checks every word.

What I'd do next

Use a range, not one ad. One comedy ad is not a standard. I would measure 3 to 5 natural ads and aim for a range, for example a typical pause between 0.35 and 0.5 seconds.

Treat the score as home-made. The scale for each measure is our own choice. It also misses breaths, small timing wobbles, and a voice warming up across a sentence. Someone could still call this voice robotic.

Confirm before investors see it. Some claims in the film come from partners and still need confirming. The film has not been tested on investors yet.

An average can hide the failure. The run-on take matched the ad's melody almost exactly, so its overall score looked fine. Checking each measure on its own is what caught it.
