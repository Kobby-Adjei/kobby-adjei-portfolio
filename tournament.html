Case two · AI evaluation · FlmLnk

Which model picks the best clip?

FlmLnk turns long videos into short clips worth posting. A frontier model chose the moments, and it was the most expensive call in the product. The question was whether something cheaper could do it without anyone noticing a drop in quality.

Answering that honestly meant not trusting my own eye. I scored every candidate without knowing which model produced it, and only opened the mapping after the last score was saved.

Step one: write down what "good" means

You cannot benchmark taste you haven't defined. So before touching a model I had our editor review 64 clips blind and score them, then read his comments back until the pattern showed itself. It was not subtle. Ten of his notes said a version of the same thing:

Must end on a statement, not mid statement. One of ten near-identical notes about endings

That became the first rule of the rubric, and it turned out to decide almost the whole tournament: a clip has to end on a finished sentence. Narrative suspense is fine — grammatical truncation is not. Ending on a cliffhanger reads as deliberate; ending mid-word reads as broken.

The rubric, and how the scale was anchoredlayer two

Priorities, in order. 1 — Ending quality is decisive; the payoff, punchline or resolved thought must land. 2 — The start must be understandable; no dropping the viewer mid-sentence. 3 — It must be an actual moment: a peak, reveal, joke, reaction or turn, not merely coherent information. 4 — Emotional rhythm is part of the moment; don't cut on the important word when the reaction completes it. 5 — Music needs the hook, not a random verse. 6 — Duplicates are scored on their own merits; repetition is a separate concern. 7 — Visual dependence is metadata, never an automatic penalty.

Scale. 4 = publishable as selected · 3 = strong moment, one small boundary fix away · 2 = useful idea, materially incomplete · 1 = no worthwhile moment.

Anchoring. One human decision in the calibration set was thrown out before I used it — a duplicate clip scored zero purely because the reviewer had already seen it twice. Fatigue is not a quality signal, and leaving it in would have taught the rubric to punish repetition.

Step two: judge blind, then unseal

Every candidate was stripped of its model name, shuffled, and scored one at a time. Reputation is the enemy of a fair benchmark — knowing a clip came from a frontier model is worth about half a point of unearned credit, and I did not trust myself to ignore it.

The result was a field that mostly agreed with itself. Nearly every model found the same good moments: the same confession in the drama reel, the same prayer in the boxing stream, the same "I made zero dollars" reveal in the documentary. What separated them was where they cut.

The number I'd defend

Roughly 76–80% lower cost per usable clip than the GPT-4o baseline, at a higher blind quality score. Measured two independent ways — per usable clip across all 297 candidates, and per completed case on six frozen videos — landing within three points of each other.

Availability findings, kept separate from qualitylayer two

A model that refuses or fails is not a model that scored badly, and mixing the two produces a dishonest leaderboard. Four routes never returned anything at all because of a data-retention policy mismatch — recorded as unavailable, never as editorial zeros.

Two OpenAI-family models refused the same video: a raunchy game show. That is a reproducible content-policy boundary, not bad luck — the newest one refused it four runs in a row. Any architecture built on those models needs a second model for that lane.

One newcomer had genuinely good taste and unusable behaviour: 28 seconds per video, one reply that rambled for four minutes and then broke mid-sentence, at eight times the cost of the leader. It was cut on operations, not on judgement.

Step three: the part I got wrong

With a leader identified, I put ten head-to-head pairs in front of our editor as actual rendered video. Seven of his ten notes complained about endings again. But something was off — he was rejecting clips I had scored as clean, and rejecting both sides of a pair on the same grounds.

do not re — both said that instead of return The note that broke the case open

The clip was supposed to end on the line "And do not return." It was ending on "And do not retur—". So I pulled the source audio and measured it.

  • The transcript said the line ended at 237.44 seconds. Every model cut there. Correctly — that is what the transcript told them.
  • The word actually ends at 237.86 seconds. The speech-to-text boundary had landed 0.42 seconds early, inside the word.
  • There is a 0.10-second dip in the middle of "return". The audio finisher's silence detector treated that dip as the end of speech and cut there — the exact gap that caused the problem.
  • The previous fix padded by 0.12 seconds. It needed 0.42. It could never have worked.

So four of the ten comparisons had been decided by a rendering bug rather than by editorial judgement — including the one testing whether a cheap model matched an expensive one, which came back "neither" because both clips were chopped identically. The models were right. The transcript was wrong. The cutter believed the transcript.

Music was worse, and failed differently. The word "facts" was rendering as "fa", and no amount of silence-hunting would find it: a -cts release is high-frequency and low-energy, invisible under a bass-heavy master — and that track never goes quiet, so a pause-seeking rule runs forever.

The corrected finisher, and how it was verifiedlayer two

The rule. From the model's chosen end, walk forward over sound — broadband energy or high-frequency sibilance — skipping gaps shorter than 0.15 s, and stop at the first real pause. Add a 0.08 s tail. Cap the extension at 0.60 s. Allow 0.30 s of slack past the next transcript segment, because the transcript boundary itself carries about 0.4 s of error. Detect continuous music by dynamic range under 20 dB and switch to tracking the vocal only.

Verification. On the drama clip, high-frequency energy in the final 100 ms fell from −14.8 dB to −31.5 dB — the word now completes and the audio settles before the clip ends. On the music clip, the sibilant ratio fell from −9.0 dB (still ringing at the cut) to −19.7 dB.

The part that matters most. On four of the twenty clips the tool refuses to help: the audio runs straight from the chosen end into the next thought with no pause anywhere, so no trim can finish it. Padding those would just bolt on half of the next sentence. Those are labelled model selection failures and left untouched. A tool that hides a model's mistakes is worse than no tool.

Where it stands

The re-render is built and waiting: the same models, the same chosen moments, corrected cuts only, with pair order and sides reshuffled so the previous session can't bias the result. It isolates model quality from render quality and costs nothing to run.

I have not declared a winner, and that is deliberate. The human result is currently unstable and confounded by the rendering bug. The candidate-level repair pass I built went one win, one loss and two ties against unrepaired clips while consuming 37% of the run's budget — so on this evidence it has not earned its place, and saying otherwise would be inventing a conclusion the data doesn't support.

What I'd want before deciding

Ten pairs is directional, not statistical. The cheap-versus-expensive question is still open because the one comparison testing it was ruined by the render. And a music case with no detectable pause remains the weakest lane in the whole system.

The lesson I actually took: most of the effort in an evaluation goes into making sure you are measuring the thing you think you are measuring. For a week the numbers said the models were failing at endings. Half of that was a tenth of a second of audio.
