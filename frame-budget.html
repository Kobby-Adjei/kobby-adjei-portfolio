How many frames does a model need to judge a film?

FlmLnk ranks buried catalog films by how worth a cheap marketing test they are. A vision model looks at a few trailer frames and a short synopsis, and picks the better of two films. Every frame it reads costs money, and the ranking compares every pair, so the frame count matters at catalog scale.

So I ran it as an ablation. Move one thing, the number of frames, hold everything else fixed, and find the point where more frames stop helping. The answer was cheaper than expected, and it was not the footage doing the work.

Step one: define what good means

There is no correct ranking of films, so the model is graded against people, not against my opinion. A team of reviewers voted on the same films through a blind app, picking the better of two at a time. Eighteen pairs reached a clear consensus. The model's score is how often its pick matches the team's pick on those pairs.

Because a forced choice between two films is right half the time by luck, fifty percent is the coin-flip line. Everything is read against that floor.

The design

One variable moves: the number of trailer frames, at 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16. Everything else is frozen, the same model, the same prompt, the same films. The smaller counts are pulled from the same 16-frame set, so going from four frames to eight only adds frames, it never swaps them.

  1. Two arms. One with the synopsis, one with frames only. The frames-only arm is the control. If it barely moves as frames increase, the model is judging from the text, not the footage, and no claim about frames would be honest without showing that.
  2. Three repeats per setting. The model is not perfectly deterministic, so each setting runs three times. The spread across the three is the noise floor.
  3. Graded against consensus. Every ranking is scored on the eighteen pairs the team agreed on, not on a number the model reports about itself.

Thirty full rankings in total, twelve thousand six hundred pairwise comparisons.

The result

Agreement climbs sharply from one frame to four, then flattens. Four, eight and sixteen frames land in the same place, inside the noise. The knee is at four frames. The frames-only line stays far below the one with a synopsis and never clears the low forties, no matter how many frames it sees.

What it means

Two findings hold above the noise. Four frames is enough, so the engine can send four and stop. And the synopsis carries more signal than the footage, so effort spent improving the text the model receives is likely to pay off more than sending it more pictures. A single frame is worse than a coin flip: one still misleads.

The footage is a supporting signal, not the main one. The story is what the model is really reading. The finding the control arm made possible

The honest ceiling

Even at its best the model matches the team about fifty-five percent of the time, a little above a coin flip on these pairs. That is a real limit, and stating it is the point. The engine is useful triage, it narrows a catalog so people test smartly, but it is not yet a judge you would trust on its own.

The instrument's resolution

The ground truth is eighteen pairs, so one pair flipping moves the score by more than five points. The two findings above are safe because their gaps are bigger than that. The finer differences between four, eight and sixteen frames are not, and I say so rather than reading a knee that the data cannot support. More votes tighten the ruler, and the study reruns on it.
