Which model can actually teach?

I build syncs, a place to practise coding interviews with a voice tutor called Blink. Choosing the model behind a tutor is usually a question of speed and price. I wanted to know something harder: put in front of someone who is stuck, which model finds where they are stuck and gets them out?

That is what a good human teacher does, and it is the part of AI in education I most wanted to understand. So I built the test a teacher would have to pass, and ran twenty-five models through it.

Step one: define what good means

A tutor that answers correctly and a tutor that teaches are different things. Research on human tutoring keeps finding the same thing: the gain comes less from good explanations than from the tutor locating what this one learner misunderstands, and getting them to do the repair.

So the study measures diagnosis, not eloquence. The thing to find is the wrong idea underneath the visible mistake. Someone whose binary search hangs will blame the loop condition. The real trouble is that they believe the middle always lands strictly between the two ends.

The design

Three stages, each narrower and harder than the last, so the expensive test is only spent on models that earned it. First speed and tool use on twenty-five models. Then twelve of them against nine learners with fixed lines. Then seven against learners who are alive.

  1. A learner that reacts. A third model plays the learner from a card the tutor never sees: the real bug, the false belief underneath, a wrong theory to push, and what would make it click. Fixed lines cannot test diagnosis, because nothing the tutor does changes them.
  2. A learner that behaves like a person. It takes in only the start of a long turn. It never pretends to follow. It never asks for a picture. It tires of being quizzed. And some learners will not work things out in their head: asked to, they ask to be shown.
  3. Two blind judges from different makers. They read every tutor on the same learner side by side, against the card, without knowing which model is which. One house style of teaching does not get to mark its own work.
  4. An answer key that was run, not reasoned. A tutor is marked down for a false statement about the code, so every claim in the key about what the code does came from running it.

The same instructions and the same eleven tools for every model. Nothing tuned per model.

The result

The fastest model to act was ninth of twelve at teaching. Then the ranking changed again when the learner could react. The model that came fifth with scripted learners came first with live ones. It was the only one that kept its turns short.

What it means

Three findings hold across the stages. Fast is not the same as good at teaching, so choosing a voice tutor on speed alone picks the wrong model. How the learner is modelled changes the ranking, so a benchmark with fixed lines rewards rich explanations that a real listener would lose. And every model reaches for the fix: most found the bug, but in 38 to 83 percent of verdicts the tutor stated or typed the repair before the learner understood why.

Knowing the answer was never the bottleneck. Withholding it was. What the live learners made visible

It changed the product more than it changed the model. Blink's instructions now cap how long a turn runs, put a picture up before a value is spoken, and answer "I have no idea" by finding where the learner got lost instead of handing over the answer.

The honest ceiling

No model took a beginner from zero to able. On a ladder of six things a learner must be able to do, the best reached one and a half rungs in twelve turns, and two of the eight stuck learners defeated all seven models.

What this cannot claim

The learners and the judges are models, so nothing here measures whether a person learned. The live learner takes in about forty words of a turn; that number is my design choice, and the finding that short turns win follows partly from it. The last stage ran each learner once, so six of eight against four of eight is suggestive, not settled. And no skilled human tutor faced the same simulated learner, so the ladder may be measuring a harsh test as much as weak tutors. The next step is small and cheap: a few real learners rating a blind sample of the same conversations.
